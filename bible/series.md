# TSUMUJI / つむじ — SERIES BIBLE

*the crown whorl; the one part of yourself you will never see*

**55 chapters. One complete story. Chapters are 26-28 pages.**
This file expands `bible/pillars.md`. Nothing here may contradict one line of it.

---

## LOGLINE

Everyone in Hanazono-chō knows Naruse Tōma's face. It has been strapped to a utility pole
outside a barbershop window, and pasted at all four exits of the station, at three times life
size, since he was fifteen — the cram-school chain that bought it renews the contract every
March. Nobody has looked at *him* in two years. Komori Nagi is invisible at school on purpose
and sovereign three stations away in her family's two-chair barbershop, where she has held
scissors since she was eight, is better than her father, and cannot legally touch a stranger's
head. He walks in five minutes before closing. His own face is lit from below on the pole
outside the glass, six feet from where they are standing. She never looks at it. She asks how
short.

**TAGLINE:** There's one part of you that you will never see. She sees it every three weeks.

## TONE MANIFESTO — TEN RULES, TEN TESTS

Every rule has a test an editor can actually run on a finished breakdown. A chapter that fails
a test goes back. No exceptions, no "it works anyway."

**1. Warmth is the load-bearing wall. Humour is the paint.**
Gentle comedy runs through every chapter — the asides, the chibi beats, the regulars — but no
chapter is *built* on a gag.
**TEST:** Strike every joke out of the breakdown. If the chapter stops working, the chapter was
a gag. Rewrite it.

**2. Feelings live in objects, chores, and hands.**
Nobody in this book announces an interior state. They discuss the length, the water temperature,
the till, the towel.
**TEST:** Any line that names an emotion must be rewritable as an instruction about a physical
thing within arm's reach. If it cannot be, cut the line — not the moment.

**3. Nobody explains themselves.**
Motive is inferred by the reader, never supplied by the character.
**TEST:** No character may state *why* they did something in the same chapter they did it. If a
reason must be said aloud, it may only be said by someone who has it wrong.

**4. Nobody is a villain, and nobody is the butt.**
Everyone is doing their honest best from where they stand — the father, the parents, the
classmate, the chain salon, the ward inspector.
**TEST:** For every character who obstructs a lead, write down the one sentence from their side
that is simply true. If you cannot write it, you have built a strawman. Delete and rebuild.

**5. Specificity is the weapon.**
Not "a shop." Ōgiya, third Saturday, 08:00, ¥3,000 with the senior discount, smells of rush
grass. Not "food." A ¥90 potato korokke from Nikuya Hirai, eaten cold, standing up, in three
bites.
**TEST:** Every chapter contains at least **three things with a proper name** and **at least one
price, time, or measurement said out loud.**

**6. The quiet is the meal. Plot is seasoning.**
At most one external development per chapter, and it must pay a character beat, not a story beat.
**TEST:** At least **eight consecutive pages** per chapter contain no external development at
all — just two people, a room, and a task.

**7. Every chapter ends on a pull. Never on peril.**
A held breath, a small hook, an unanswered line, a door not yet opened. Never danger, never a
cliff, never a shout.
**TEST:** The last page must contain an unanswered question, and that question must not be "will
someone be hurt." If the final page holds its tension even though the reader knows everyone is
fine, it passes.

**8. Between the two of them there is only the unsaid, never the mistaken.**
External misreadings are legal. The leads never misunderstand each other for longer than a page.
**TEST:** Any conflict between Nagi and Tōma must be resolvable by one of them saying one true
sentence. If it needs a coincidence, a missed message, or an overheard half-line, it is a
contrivance. Cut it.

**9. The craft is correct at full speed.**
The barbering is real. A working barber must be able to read a cut scene without wincing.
**TEST:** If a step in the sequence is skipped on the page, a *character* notices it out loud.
Unnoticed shortcuts are errors, not style.

**10. Titles land last.**
A title must mean one thing on page 1 and a different, heavier thing on the last page.
**TEST:** Read the title cold, then read it after the chapter. If it means the same thing both
times, retitle the chapter.

## THE WORLD — HANAZONO-CHŌ (花園町)

An ordinary outer-ward neighbourhood. Not quaint, not shabby. A place with a bathhouse still
open, three empty shopfronts, and a shopping street that fills at 17:40 and empties by 20:30.
Two-storey shophouses with the family living above the trade. Everything below is drawable and
walkable, and the distances are fixed.

### The line and the station

**THE TOBIUME LINE (飛梅線)**, run by Tobiume Electric Railway — a private commuter line, four-car
trains, every six minutes at rush hour and every twelve midday. It runs elevated through
Hanazono-chō, so the shopping street lives partly in its shadow and the 21:04 shakes the licence
frames on the barbershop wall. Hanazono-chō is the eleventh stop from the terminus. Kōyō High
School is not on the line; you walk.

**HANAZONO-CHŌ STATION** has one small elevated concourse and **four exits**. There is a poster
board inside each exit's ticket hall, and Naruse Tōma's face — same photograph, same slight
smile — has been on all four since he was fifteen, weathering at four different rates.

1. **KITA-GUCHI — "the Bus Mouth."** North. Bus rotary, taxi rank, a covered smoking corner, the
   ward noticeboard. The largest board and the newest-looking poster: it is under cover, and he
   has not aged a day here.
2. **MINAMI-GUCHI — "the Arcade Mouth."** South. Opens straight onto Hanazono Ginza. Busiest
   exit. This poster sits behind scratched acrylic; somebody has scratched a tiny moustache into
   the acrylic, not the paper, and it can only be seen from the left.
3. **NISHI-GUCHI — "the Bicycle Exit" (charinko-guchi).** West. Four hundred bicycles in racks,
   the river path beyond. Half open to the weather. **This is the poster whose top-left corner
   lifts in the October typhoon and is never re-stuck.** It is the one Tōma checks.
4. **HIGASHI-GUCHI — "the School Exit."** East. Narrow, one staircase, no lift, and every Kōyō
   student in the ward comes through it between 07:35 and 07:50. This poster is at eye height on
   the stair landing, so eight hundred students pass his face at arm's length twice a day and
   none of them see it any more.

### Hanazono Ginza (花園銀座商店街)

About forty shops. A steel-frame arcade roof for the first 180 metres out of Minami-guchi, then
open sky. Named shops, all of which recur:

- **TATAMI ŌGIYA (畳 扇屋)** — tatami maker, est. 1948, third generation. The doorway smells of
  rush grass and you can smell it from the pavement. Ōgiya shuts at noon on the third Saturday of
  the month, which is the only reason the shop's oldest regular can be in chair one at 08:00. He
  has sat in it every third Saturday for forty years.
- **KAWASE SEIKA-TEN (川瀬青果店)** — greengrocer. Crates out to the kerb, hand-lettered price
  cards in red marker, mandarins by the netted bag in winter, ¥380.
- **NIKUYA HIRAI (精肉 平井)** — butcher with a fryer in the front window. Potato korokke ¥90,
  menchi-katsu ¥140, both wrapped in paper that goes translucent. The shop's default dinner.
- **TSUKEMONO MARUKO (漬物マルコ)** — pickles out of wooden barrels, free taste on a toothpick.
  The old woman who runs it never remembers a face and always remembers what you buy.
- **KISSA TOMOSHIBI (喫茶 ともしび)** — a coffee shop undecorated since 1979. Morning set until
  11:00 (toast, egg, coffee, ¥520). Cream soda ¥480. The master polishes one glass for the length
  of an entire conversation.
- **PAN NO MIMI (パンのみみ)** — bakery. Melonpan out of the oven at 15:40 and gone by 15:55.
  Sells the day's crusts in a bag for ¥50, which is what the shop's name means.
- **BUNGUDŌ ISHIZUKA (文具 石塚)** — stationer. **This is where the appointment book's pencils
  come from: 2B, sold singly over the counter, ¥90.** Also the ward's supply of exam stationery,
  application-form envelopes, and ¥400 graduation photo frames.
- **HANAZONO-YU (花園湯)** — the bathhouse. Closed Thursdays. The mural is a mountain nobody in the
  ward has been able to identify and everybody has an opinion about.
- **HANAZONO ICHIBAN PHARMACY (花園一番薬局)** — chemist. The only air conditioning on the street
  in August, so people go in and buy nothing.
- **ICHIRIN (一輪生花店)** — florist at the arcade mouth. Buckets on the pavement. In late December
  the pine-and-bamboo New Year arrangements take over half the frontage and the whole street
  smells of cut pine for nine days.
- **YOSHIMI SOBA (よし美そば)** — standing soba, no chairs. Kake ¥420, tanuki ¥480. Open until
  21:00, later than anywhere else, which is where you eat when you closed at eight.
- **ARAI COIN LAUNDRY (あらいコインランドリー)** — 24 hours, six machines, three of them reliable.
  Where the shop's towels go when the machine in the back room dies, which it does.

### The river, the route, and the twenty minutes

**THE ASAGI RIVER (浅葱川)** runs behind the west side of town, four metres below street level
between concrete banks, a paved path along the north side. Cherry trees on the far bank only — a
planting decision from 1974 that people still complain about. A low weir 300 metres downstream of
Kotori Bridge makes a constant white noise you stop hearing after a week and hear again the
moment you are unhappy. Three crossings: **KOTORI BRIDGE (小鳥橋)**, painted green, with the good
railing to lean on; **NAKABASHI (中橋)**, concrete, carries the road; **SHIMO-NO-HASHI (下ノ橋)**,
the low one, which floods to ankle depth twice a year, about which the ward puts out a sign
nobody reads.

**BARBER KOMORI** is *not* on the arcade. It stands forty metres off it, on the corner where
Hanazono Ginza meets the river road, facing the utility pole. This matters: quiet enough after
19:00 to hear the striped pole's motor from inside, public enough that anyone walking from the
arcade to the river path passes the window.

**KŌYŌ HIGH SCHOOL** — twenty minutes on foot from Higashi-guchi, and they are the same twenty
minutes for eight hundred people, which is why Nagi leaves ninety seconds after the bell and is
alone by minute four.

**THE TWENTY-MINUTE WALK, timed** (Higashi-guchi to the school gate):

- **0:00** — the stair landing, past his face at eye height.
- **1:30** — the crossing at the Nakabashi road. The signal is 55 seconds and everyone knows it.
- **3:00** — the vending machine outside the shuttered rice merchant. The corn soup button has
  said SOLD OUT since March. Somebody presses it anyway every single morning.
- **5:00** — the long grey wall of the waterworks. Two hundred metres, nothing to look at, which
  is exactly why every real conversation on this route happens here.
- **8:00** — **the Nine-Percent**: the hill. A real gradient, four minutes, and the point where
  the crowd sorts itself by fitness and by who is talking to whom.
- **12:00** — the hedge and the shrine steps. The last flat stretch.
- **15:00** — the convenience store. Last chance. ¥110 pork bun in winter.
- **17:00** — the gate in sight, and the final two hundred metres where everyone runs and Nagi
  does not, because she left first.

## BARBER KOMORI (理容コモリ) — THE FLOOR PLAN

Nine metres deep, four and a half wide. Every named object below is a prop for feelings and is
available to any writer, forever.

**Outside.** A **striped pole** on a bracket at shoulder height. The casing cracked in 2011 and
the motor has turned at the wrong speed ever since — noticeably slow, a half-beat behind what
your eye expects. It has never been fixed. Nagi's father says he will get to it; her grandmother
says it is fine; nobody agrees on which way it turned before.

Two metres to the left, at the kerb: the **utility pole**, with a narrow vertical advertising
panel strapped to it at head height. **SEIKA SEMINAR — a boy's face, three times life size, a
slight smile.** It has been there for two years. The streetlamp above it is on a photocell, so at
closing time that face is the brightest thing on the corner. Nobody who works in this shop has
ever looked at it.

**The door and the bell.** Glass in an aluminium frame, the shop name in gold-edged lettering —
except that the **MORI** was re-lettered in 2003 after a delivery van clipped it, in a very
slightly different gold, and you can see the join if you know. Inside the frame, a **brass bell
on a bent strip.** It gives two notes: a bright one always, and a second, lower one that only
sounds if the door is pushed hard. Nagi's grandmother is the only person in the family who
reliably makes it ring twice. *(Her name is Suzu — bell. Nobody in the family has ever made a
joke about that, which is how you know it means something.)*

**Chair one** — hydraulic, 1978, reupholstered twice, the last time in a maroon vinyl that was
the cheap option. The left armrest is wrapped in black tape at the wear point. The pump pedal
sticks in cold weather and needs two kicks. The linoleum around it carries a faint compass-rose
scuff, worn by fifty years of a barber pivoting on one heel, and you cannot see it unless the low
sun comes in at about 17:20 in November.

**Chair two** — 1961, cast-iron base, original leather, cracked at the seam on the right where
somebody's belt buckle sat for thirty years. **The grandmother's chair.** Wiped down every Sunday
morning, the leather fed twice a year, the hydraulics kept charged. Nobody sits in it. Not
customers, not family, not visitors, not Nagi. It is not forbidden out loud; it simply never
happens, and the first time somebody tries it the whole shop notices.

**The mirror wall.** Full length behind both chairs, three panels, the middle one replaced and a
shade cleaner than its neighbours. On the shelf beneath: two spray bottles, a jar of blue
disinfectant with combs standing in it, a tub of talc, a soft **neck duster** with a wooden
handle, hair tonic in a bottle that has been three-quarters full for a decade, and a small
chipped ceramic frog nobody can explain.

**The shampoo basin.** Left wall, forward-lean type, a red-and-blue mixer tap set to a temperature
that is right and that nobody is allowed to adjust. The plug chain is a bootlace. The drain
gurgles once, ten seconds after you stop the water, every single time.

**The hot-towel cabinet.** Under the mirror shelf, capacity twelve rolled towels. It hums at a
pitch you notice only when it stops. **The door does not latch; it is held shut with a rubber band
looped over the handle**, and replacing that rubber band is a job nobody has ever been assigned.

**The wall of framed licences.** Right-hand wall, above the waiting bench, in order: Komori Suzu's
barber's licence, 1969, the photograph a young woman with an unreadable face; her husband's, 1966;
Komori Sōichi's, 1998; the ward hygiene certificate, re-slotted each year over the old one so the
frame is four sheets thick; the fire inspection card; the barbers' association plate, brass,
unpolished.

And at the end of the row, **one empty frame.** Same size, same wood, same nail. Suzu hung it the
year Nagi turned eight and has never once mentioned it.

**The waiting bench** — three seats, vinyl, a stack of manga magazines between eleven and four
years old, and a low table with a plastic ashtray recessed into it from when that was allowed.

**The till.** A Casio from 1994. **The drawer opens on the second push.** Sōichi hits it with the
heel of his hand. Nagi lifts the front edge a centimetre and pushes, and it opens first time, and
she has never told him.

**The appointment book.** A5, soft cover, one page per day ruled into half-hours, written entirely
**in pencil** — 2B from Bungudō Ishizuka, ¥90, sharpened with a blade. Names in the left column,
times in the right. Cancellations are erased, not crossed out, so every page keeps its ghosts.
**On the last ruled page at the back is a column of numbers counting down, one crossed off per
month, in Nagi's handwriting since she was fifteen.**

**The broom and the floor.** A soft-bristled barber's broom, wooden handle worn to a shine at the
grip, and a metal dustpan with a bent lip that only works held at exactly one angle. Hair goes
into a lidded bin by the back-room door. **The floor is swept between every single head**, and the
sweeping is where everyone in this series says the true things, because you are not looking at
each other while you do it.

**The back room** — three mats, curtained off. Kettle, two chipped mugs, an electric towel washer
louder than it should be, boxes of single-use #10 razor blades, three-litre jugs of shampoo, a
wall calendar from the blade supplier with a mountain on it, a stool, the household account ledger
in a plastic sleeve, and a radio on a shelf that stays on AM through the baseball season.
**A steel drawer in the workbench holds the grandmother's old fixed-blade straight razor**, which
is no longer legal to use on a customer under the single-use hygiene rules and is honed anyway,
twice a year, by somebody.

**The stairs.** Fourteen, steep, behind the back room, to the flat above. The fifth creaks. Suzu
comes down at 07:30 and goes back up at 20:40 and does not use the handrail, because using it
would be admitting something.

## THE CRAFT — THE BARBERING PRIMER

This section is law. Writers mine it for detail and obey its order. If a step is skipped on the
page, a character notices out loud.

### What actually happens, in order

**1. The consultation.** Two to five minutes, standing *behind* the seated customer, both of them
looking at the mirror instead of at each other — the single most important physical fact in this
entire series. The barber's hands go into the dry hair: lifting at the crown, pushing the fringe
back, running a thumb up the nape against the growth. You are not asking what they want. You are
finding out what the head will allow. A good consultation ends with the barber knowing something
about this person's head that they have never known themselves.

**2. The cape and the neck strip.** A paper strip laid round the neck first so the cape never
touches skin, then the nylon cape over it, snapped at the back — second snap position for thicker
necks. A towel folded into the collar. The cape is shaken out with a crack before it goes on,
folded down over the chest, and the customer's arms go *under*.

**3. The cut.** Komori cuts damp, from a spray bottle, never soaking. First the **guide section**:
one length cut in one place, against which every other hair on the head is measured for the next
thirty minutes. Then the working sections, clipped up and taken down in order — back, sides, top —
each panel combed, held between middle and index finger, cut against the fingers. The comb travels
ahead of the shears the whole way; the shears never hunt. **Point-cutting** into the ends to soften
a line. **Slide-cutting** to move weight without losing length. **Thinning shears** (30-tooth,
roughly fifteen per cent per pass) used two or three times and never near the ends, where they
leave a spike you will see in a fortnight. **Clippers** up the nape — corded, lever open, blade at
0.8 mm, guards at 3, 6, 9, 12 mm — with the scoop of the wrist at the top of the stroke that makes
the difference between a fade and a line. Then the **corner** is taken off around the ear with the
tip of the shears, which is the move that separates a barber from a person holding scissors.

**4. The shave and the neckline.** Lather warmed and worked in with a brush in a circle, then the
**straight razor** — a folding handle with a **single-use #10 blade**, snapped in fresh in front of
the customer so they hear it. Around the ears, the sideburn line, down the nape. On a full service,
the face: with the grain first, across it second, never against it on a young face. This is the
barber's legal monopoly and the series treats it as such — the razor is the line, and everybody in
this shop knows exactly where the line is.

**5. The shampoo.** *After* the cut, not before. Forward-lean basin, water tested on the inside of
the barber's own wrist, one hand always between the water and the customer's forehead. Two passes,
the second shorter. Fingertips, never nails. The customer's eyes are closed and their whole face is
exposed and they have no idea what expression they are making, which is a fact this series uses
more than once.

**6. The hot towel.** Out of the cabinet, unrolled, snapped once in the air to bring it down from
*too hot* to *exactly right*, then laid over the face from the chin upward and pressed at the
temples and the bridge of the nose with two hands. Four to six seconds of pressure. This is the
shop's small ceremony and the series' most loaded object: **who wraps whom, and how long they leave
it on.**

**7. The finish (shiage).** Towel-dry; blow-dry with the comb leading, in the direction of growth
and never against it. Product, if any, warmed in the palms first. The **neck duster** and talc along
the hairline, into the collar, across the shoulders. The cape comes off with one hand at the neck
and one gathering the hair inward so nothing spills. A brush down the back.

**8. The mirror-check.** The hand mirror held up behind the head at an angle so the customer sees
the back of their own skull in the wall mirror — **reversed, for about two seconds, at the wrong
distance, and never again until next time.** That is the closest any person ever gets to seeing
their own crown. The barber has been looking steadily at it for forty minutes.

### The vocabulary

**Kenagare (毛流れ)** — the growth pattern; the direction each region actually wants to lie.
**Tsumuji (つむじ)** — the crown whorl. Roughly four in five turn clockwise; the rest do not, and a
counter-clockwise whorl set left of the crown throws a fringe to one side forever no matter what
you do to it. Some heads have two, and two whorls is a life sentence.
**Hane (跳ね)** — the cowlick; the kick; the bit at the back that stands up on day nine.
**Mōryō (毛量)** — density: how much hair per square centimetre, which is not the same as thickness.
**Katai / yawarakai** — coarse or soft. **Eriashi (襟足)** — the nape line, and its three shapes:
**kaku** (square), **maru** (round), **shizen** (natural, tapered). **The weight line** — the
horizontal band where the bulk sits; move it up and the head reads lighter, move it down and it
reads heavier, and almost nobody outside the trade knows this was a choice somebody made.
**Graduation** — length increasing or decreasing across a stacked section. **Over-direction** —
pulling a section away from where it grows to build length into a shape. **Karui / omoi** — light or
heavy: the two words customers actually use.

### What makes a cut good

This section explains why Nagi is extraordinary. Writers may not contradict it.

A mediocre cut looks correct on the day it is cut. **A good cut looks correct in week three.**
Anyone can make a head look right in a mirror with wet hair and a dryer in their hand. The craft is
cutting for a head you will not be standing behind: for how this hair grows out, for how it falls on
the fourth morning when this person has had four hours of sleep and ninety seconds to deal with it.

Concretely, a good barber:

- **cuts to the growth, not to the picture.** You find the whorl, the cowlick, the flat spot at the
  back of the skull, and you *let them do what they were going to do anyway*, and shape around them.
  Fighting a whorl costs the customer a fortnight of mornings.
- **puts weight where the skull is flat and takes it off where the skull is round.** Every head has
  both. Nobody's head is a sphere.
- **cuts for the person's actual hands.** Can they reproduce this in ninety seconds with wet hands
  and no product? If not, it is a photograph, not a haircut.
- **cuts to the face's asymmetry.** Everyone's face is asymmetric. A symmetrical haircut on an
  asymmetric face is what makes people say *I look strange and I do not know why.*
- **cuts the nape for the collar the person actually wears.** A school collar, a work shirt, a round
  neck: a different line for each.
- **does not correct.** This is the whole thing. A skilled barber is not fast because they hurry;
  they are fast because every cut they make is the cut they meant. Watch an ordinary barber and you
  will see them go back over one section four times, taking a millimetre, checking, taking a
  millimetre. Watch Nagi and the shears go through a section once.

**The line the series lives on: *a good barber does not cut hair twice.*** Her father is a very good
barber who corrects. She does not. Everyone in the shop, including him, knows what that means, and
nobody has said it in three years.

### The law, as this series treats it

- Cutting hair for payment requires a **barber's licence**. The route is fixed: a designated
  **barber training school** — two years full-time day course, or three years part-time — then the
  **national examination**, written and practical, sat twice a year; then registration; then the
  licence. Only a licensed barber may put a razor to a customer's face. That is the legal bright
  line between a barber and everybody else.
- There is **no minimum age in the law itself**. The real gate is that you cannot enter a training
  school without finishing high school. Nagi's wall is not her age. It is her *school year*.
- **Applications for the April intake open on 1 June of your final school year.** From chapter 1
  that date is **fourteen months away**, and it is the number counting down in pencil in the back of
  the appointment book. It reaches zero in chapter 44.
- She says "fourteen months." Fourteen months is the distance to the **door**, not to the licence —
  the licence is three and a half years out, and she knows that perfectly well, and says the smaller
  number because the bigger one is unsayable. Writers: never correct her.
- **The risk is not hers alone, and that is the point.** An unlicensed person cutting a paying
  customer exposes **the shop**: Sōichi's licence, the premises registration, the ward hygiene
  approval, the family's income. It is not a private secret with private stakes. It is a
  seventeen-year-old holding her father's whole life in her right hand every time she picks up the
  shears, choosing it anyway, weekly — and being *right* to, and that being no defence at all.
- Cutting **family** is legal. Cutting **for free** is a grey area the trade knows is not as grey as
  it would like. Cutting a paying stranger is not grey.

Money in this series is never a crisis and never a speech. It is a laminated price list on the
mirror, a ledger in a plastic sleeve, and a number somebody does in their head while sweeping.

### The number nobody has said out loud

On a Saturday when Nagi is at chair one, Barber Komori does sixteen to nineteen heads. Sōichi
alone does twelve. That difference — about five heads, about ¥19,000 — happens roughly every
Saturday of the year, plus the New Year week, plus Golden Week.

**Just under ¥1.2 million a year of this shop's turnover exists because an unlicensed
seventeen-year-old is standing at chair one.** That is about twelve per cent. Take it away and
the shop is not ruined; it is only tight, forever. Her father has never done this arithmetic
aloud, and the reader should do it for him somewhere around chapter 21.

## THE CALENDAR — 55 CHAPTERS ACROSS TWO SCHOOL YEARS

The story runs from the second week of April in their **second year** to the middle of March at
the end of their **third**. Twenty-four months, fifty-five chapters — and deliberately not
evenly.

**Thirty-seven chapters cover the second year. Eighteen cover the third.** That is the design.
The year you meet someone is long and made of weeks; the year you decide things is short and made
of months. Arc 3 in particular is eleven chapters across one winter, because by then they see
each other every few days and the intervals have collapsed. Arc 5 is ten chapters across eight
months, and it accelerates, and that is what the last year of school actually feels like.

### The school's fixed year (Kōyō High School)

- **April** — opening ceremony, new class assignment, the first careers survey of the year.
- **Mid-May** — mid-term exams.
- **Early July** — end-of-term exams. Summer holiday from about the 20th.
- **1 September** — second term. Sports festival at the end of the month.
- **Second weekend of October** — the **Kōyō culture festival**. Two days; the second is open to
  the public, which is why half the ward is on the street that Sunday.
- **November** — the third-years go quiet. You can hear it in a corridor.
- **Late December to early January** — winter holiday.
- **Mid-January** — the national common test. In Year A it belongs to the year above; in Year B it
  is theirs.
- **Late February** — entrance examinations.
- **Early March** — graduation, and then a hollow fortnight in which nobody knows what to do with
  their hands.

### The shop's year

- **26-31 DECEMBER — the pre-New-Year rush. The biggest week in barbering, everywhere, always.**
  Nobody in this ward starts a year with last year's haircut. Barber Komori opens at 08:00 and
  shuts when the queue ends, which on the 30th is nearer 22:00. **No appointments are taken on
  the 30th or the 31st** — first come, and the line starts on the pavement before the shutter is
  up. Six days, roughly **190 heads and ¥730,000**: an entire ordinary month, compressed. The
  towel cabinet cannot keep up. Ichirin's pine takes over the arcade. Suzu comes downstairs.
- **29 April - 5 May, Golden Week** — the second run. Fathers at home, children's cuts, fourteen
  heads a day.
- **15 November, Shichi-Go-San** — first haircuts, formal photographs, mothers who cry and are
  embarrassed about it. **Since 1969 this shop has kept every baby's first lock in a small paper
  envelope with the date written on it.** There is a shoebox of them upstairs. Nagi's is in there.
- **Late June to August — the buzz-cut season.** The baseball club, the clippers, ¥3,800 for
  eleven minutes' work, and a bin that has to be emptied twice a day.
- **June and September — the rainy months.** Every head in the ward misbehaves; the towel cabinet
  fights the damp; the dehumidifier bucket in the back room gets emptied at 14:00 and 19:00.
- **14 August** — the only whole day of the year the shop is shut and the family does nothing.
- **February — the slowest month of the year.** Two heads before noon. This is when the accounts
  get done, at the waiting bench, with the radio off.
